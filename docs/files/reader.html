A Silence of Three Parts

IT WAS NIGHT AGAIN. The Waystone Inn lay in silence, and it was a silence of three parts.

The most obvious part was a hollow, echoing quiet, made by things that were lacking. If there had been a wind it would have sighed through the trees, set the inn's sign creaking on its hooks, and brushed the silence down the road like trailing autumn leaves. If there had been a crowd, even a handful of men inside the inn, they would have filled the silence with conversation and laughter, the clatter and clamor one expects from a drinking house during the dark hours of night.

The second silence was in the things that were there, felt but not seen, like the movement of shadows in the flickering candlelight, or the way the barman stood behind the bar and polished a glass with a white cloth, then set it down and picked up another.

The third silence was not an easy thing to notice. If you listened for an hour, you might begin to feel it in the walls. It was in the weight of the black stone hearth that held the heat of a long dead fire. It was in the slow back-and-forth of a white cloth rubbing along the bar. It was in the hands of the man who stood there, polishing a glass with the silence of someone who has forgotten how to stop.

The Waystone was his, just as the silence was his. This was appropriate, as it was the greatest silence of the three, wrapping the others inside itself. It was deep and wide as autumn. It was the patient, cut-flower sound of a man who is waiting to die.

The barman's name was Kote. He had chosen the name carefully when he had come to this place. He had chosen it the way other men might choose a piece of clothing to wear. He had chosen it because names were important to him, and because it was the name a broken man might choose.
